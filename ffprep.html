Key facts to know

Founded: Around 1870 - one of AR's oldest departments. Went fully career in the 1970s.

ISO Rating: Class 1 - the best possible. Only about 4% of U.S. departments earn this.

Strategic Plan: 2024-2029 plan adopted April 2024. Focus: continuous improvement and CPSE accreditation. Theme: "Service is our why."

Fire Chief: Brad Hardin

HQ / Station 1: 303 W. Center Street - on the National Register of Historic Places

Shifts: A, B, and C Shift (24-on / 48-off schedule)

EMS system: FFD provides BLS first response. Washington County Regional Ambulance Authority (Central EMS) transports to hospitals. All apparatus carry AEDs.

Specialized teams - know at least 2 of these

Technical Rescue Team (TRT): 45 members. Rope rescue, confined space, trench rescue, structural collapse, heavy vehicle extrication, antenna/tower rescue. Responds regionwide across NW Arkansas.

HazMat Team: 30 technician-level members. 5 groups: Air Monitoring, Protective Equipment, Communications/Research, Decon, Spill Control. Partnered with the 61st Civil Support Team out of Little Rock.

Swift Water Rescue: Zodiac boats, wetsuits, PFDs. Critical because Fayetteville has 3 city lakes, the White River, and flood-prone areas.

Aircraft Rescue (ARFF): Station 8 on South School Ave covers the airport. All assigned firefighters are ARFF certified.

Public Education: Free CPR/AED, Stop the Bleed, and First Aid courses offered to the community - shows deep investment in prevention and public service.

Honor Guard: Est. 2002. Posts colors at funerals and ceremonies - shows the department's respect for tradition and fallen members.

Population: About 101,680 - 2nd largest city in Arkansas

University of Arkansas: Flagship campus, 34,000+ students, $3B+ annual economic impact. Dense, young, transient population = high call volume and diverse scenarios.

NW Arkansas: Fortune 500 corridor - Walmart (Bentonville), Tyson Foods (Springdale), J.B. Hunt (Lowell). One of the fastest-growing metros in the U.S.

Geography/hazards: 3 city lakes, White River, flood-prone areas, regional airport, high-density residential near campus.

SARA - structure for every story-based answer

S - Situation: Who, what, where, why. Set the scene in 1-2 sentences.

A - Action: What YOU specifically did. Use "I" not "we." Be concrete.

R - Result: What happened because of your actions. Positive outcome.

A - Application: How this applies to the fire service and this role.

Keep every answer under 3 minutes. Time yourself when you practice tonight.

Values - your personal definitions and stories

Format for every value answer: Define it in your own words → tell YOUR story (SARA) → tie it to the fire service. Under 3 minutes.

INTEGRITY — Doing the right thing when no one is watching, especially when it would be easy not to.

Your story: Roofing. When you are on someone's roof, they cannot see you. They have no idea if you are cutting corners, skipping steps, or doing it right. They are trusting you completely. That is integrity in its purest form — your standard does not change because no one is watching.

Tie-in: "In the fire service, your crew and your community trust you the same way. When you are inside a burning building, no one is standing over you checking your work. Your integrity is the only thing holding the standard."

ACCOUNTABILITY — Owning your commitments and following through even when it is hard.

Your story: College. College was not easy or enjoyable, but you knew finishing mattered — for your future and for proving to yourself you finish what you start. You held yourself accountable: attended every class, took 18 credit hours this summer to stay on track to graduate. No one forced you. You did it because you said you would.

Tie-in: "Accountability in a firehouse means your crew does not have to wonder if you will show up, do your part, or follow through. I have been proving that to myself for the last few years."

TEAMWORK — Every person has a role. When one piece fails, the whole unit fails.

Your story: Football captain. As captain, your job was not just to perform — it was to make sure every person on that team understood their role and felt it mattered. The line that never gets the glory, the backup who has to stay ready. Culture and communication were everything. When both were right, the team won. When either broke down, it showed up on the scoreboard.

Tie-in: "On a fire scene, every role is critical — the engineer, the nozzle man, the crew outside. One person freelancing or not communicating puts everyone at risk. I understand that from football in a deep way."

CUSTOMER SERVICE — Reading the person in front of you and meeting them where they are, not where you want them to be.

Your story: 5 siblings. Growing up with five siblings, every single one of them needed something different from you. Talking to Hayden about football or the future is a completely different conversation than playing with Jax, or helping Paige with her garden. You learned early that real service means adapting to the person — not giving everyone the same thing.

Tie-in: "Every call is a different person in a different kind of crisis. A firefighter who shows up with the same energy and approach for every person is not doing customer service — they are going through motions. I learned to read the room before I ever knew that was a skill."

EMPATHY — Putting yourself in someone else's position so completely that your response actually fits what they need.

Your story: Big Brother. Your Little has come to you with things that hurt — big losses, breakups, moments where he did not know what to do. You did not hand him a solution. You put yourself in his position first. You remembered what those things felt like at his age. That is what allowed your advice to actually land instead of just sounding like an adult talking at him.

Tie-in: "On an EMS call or at a fire scene, the person in front of you is not a situation to manage — they are a human being having the worst moment of their life. Empathy is what keeps you from treating them like a call number."

TRUST — Built through consistent action over time, not words. Earned on the practice field before the game.

Your story: Football again. As captain, the team did not trust you because you had the title — they trusted you because of what you did every day in practice when it did not matter. The way you handled a bad rep. The way you treated the youngest player on the roster. Trust was the scoreboard of how you showed up every single day before anyone was watching.

Tie-in: "In a firehouse, trust between crew members is built the same way — long before there is an emergency. If your crew does not trust you at the dinner table, they will not trust you inside a structure fire."

The 4 questions they will almost certainly ask
Tell us about yourself.+

What they're really asking: Who are you as a person, not a resume bullet? Are you personable, self-aware, and a good communicator?

Open: Where you're from / what shaped your character early on
Middle: Work or life experience that built relevant skills - teamwork, service, physical challenge, problem-solving
Close: Why you're sitting in that chair today - connect your story to this career

What NOT to do: Don't recite your resume. Don't list certifications. Don't open with "I've always wanted to be a firefighter."

Start with something personal and memorable - a place, a person, a defining moment. Make them lean in.
Why do you want to be a firefighter?+

What they're really asking: Is your motivation genuine and specific - or are you one of 50 people who said "I want to help people"?

Don't say: "I want to help people" or "I want to serve my community." These are true but generic and forgettable.

Do say: Tell the specific story. What lit the fire? A call you witnessed? Someone who influenced you? A moment where you realized this was the only job you wanted?

S: The specific moment, person, or event that pointed you toward this career
A: What you did AFTER that moment - how you pursued it (physical prep, certs, research, ride-alongs)
R: Where that journey brought you - sitting in front of this panel
A: What the fire service means to you: service, brotherhood, challenge, community impact
The panel wants to FEEL the why. Make them feel it, don't just say it.
What have you done to prepare for a career in the fire service?+

What they're really asking: Have you taken this seriously before anyone hired you? Are you investing in yourself?

Cover 3 areas:

Physical: Your training regimen - how you have prepared your body for the demands of the job
Mental: Interview prep, department research, studying the fire service - show you went beyond the surface
Certs and Experience: EMT, Firefighter I/II, CPR, first aid, ride-alongs, volunteer work, relevant jobs and life experiences

Life and work experiences matter as much as certs. Any job where you worked under pressure, served others in crisis, or solved physical problems counts.

Mention researching Fayetteville FD specifically - their strategic plan, specialized teams, ISO rating. Shows you went beyond a Google search.
Why do you want to work for the Fayetteville Fire Department specifically?+

What they're really asking: Did you apply here because you want THIS department, or just because it was the first posting you saw?

Community: "Fayetteville is a growing university city - the call volume and variety of scenarios is exactly the environment I want to develop in"
Specializations: TRT, HazMat, Swift Water - real opportunities to grow into specialized, high-skill roles over a career
Reputation: ISO Class 1 - only about 4% of departments earn this. That standard speaks to how seriously this department takes its craft.
Direction: The 2024-2029 Strategic Plan and CPSE accreditation pursuit - "This is a department actively investing in being better. That is where I want to grow."
If you have a personal connection to Fayetteville or NW Arkansas, mention it. Community roots matter to panels.
Values and character questions
What does integrity mean to you, and how do you display it daily?+

Define: Integrity is doing the right thing even when no one is watching - especially when it costs you something.

S: A real situation where you had to choose the harder right thing over the easier wrong thing
A: What you did - and what it cost you (a friendship, a shortcut, a benefit)
R: How it turned out - trust was earned, the outcome was better, you could hold your head up
A: In the fire service, your crew trusts you with their lives. Integrity is not optional - it is the foundation of everything.
What are three traits you would bring to this department?+

Don't just list adjectives. Pick 3 values, personally define each one, and give a quick real example for each.

Strong picks: Integrity, Accountability, Teamwork, Coachability, Work Ethic, Reliability, Empathy

Format per trait: "One trait I would bring is [X]. To me that means [personal definition]. For example, [1-sentence real story]. In the fire service, that matters because [tie-in]."
Match your traits to the department's values if you know them. Shows alignment and research.
Why is teamwork important in the fire service?+

Core answer: "We do everything together - and for specific reasons: safety, effectiveness, and because lives, property, and the environment depend on coordination."

A firefighter working alone is a firefighter in danger. Communication, trust, and coordination are not optional - they are survival.

S: A time you worked on a real team under real pressure
A: Your specific role - how you communicated, supported others, stepped up when needed
R: The outcome that would not have happened without the team
A: On a fire scene, crew cohesion saves lives. I learned that outside this job and I will carry it in.
Behavioral and situational questions
Describe a time you had a conflict with a coworker. How did you handle it?+

What they're watching for: Emotional maturity. Can you work through friction without blowing up or shutting down?

Don't say: "I don't really have conflicts" or "I just kept my head down." That reads as avoidant and immature.

S: A real disagreement where there was genuine friction - not trivial
A: How you stepped back, approached the person directly, listened, and found common ground
R: The relationship improved. The work improved. You both moved forward.
A: In a firehouse, you live with your crew for 24 hours. Friction that is left to fester affects the whole team and ultimately the community you serve.
You witness a fellow firefighter steal from a fire victim. What do you do?+

What they're really asking: Does your integrity bend under social pressure - even when the wrong person is someone on your crew?

The answer: You report it. No exceptions. No version of this where you look the other way.

Say: "The trust the community places in us is the foundation of everything we do. I would address it directly with that firefighter first - giving them the chance to self-report. Then I would report it to my supervisor. That is not betraying a coworker. That is protecting the department, the community, and every firefighter who serves with honor."

Don't waver on this. The panel is testing whether your integrity bends under social pressure. It does not.
Would you ever disobey a direct order?+

The honest answer: Almost never. The chain of command exists for life-safety reasons. Be clear and confident about this.

The one exception: If following the order would result in clear, immediate, and preventable loss of life - including your own. And even then, you communicate first.

Say: "I believe strongly in the chain of command - it exists because coordinated, disciplined teams save more lives. I would follow orders even if I disagreed, and raise concerns through the proper channels afterward. The only exception would be if an order put someone in clear and immediate danger - and even then, my first move is to communicate that concern loudly and clearly, not just act unilaterally."

Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult or irate person.+

What they're asking: Can you stay calm, professional, and empathetic when someone is at their worst?

S: A real encounter with an upset, scared, or aggressive person - in a job, at school, anywhere
A: You stayed calm. You listened first. You did not match their energy. You acknowledged their feelings before solving the problem.
R: The situation de-escalated. The person felt heard. The outcome improved.
A: At an emergency scene, people are in crisis. Meeting them with patience and empathy makes every aspect of the response go better.
What is a weakness you have, and what are you doing about it?+

Don't say: "I work too hard" or "I am a perfectionist." These are non-answers that signal a lack of self-awareness.

Do say: A real, honest weakness that does not disqualify you from the job, but shows self-awareness and a growth plan.

Examples: "Early in my career I struggled to ask for help - I thought it showed weakness. I've learned that in the fire service, knowing when to ask IS competence. I have been deliberately working on this by [specific action]."

Or: "I used to get too focused on a task and lose sight of the bigger picture. I have been practicing stepping back and checking in with my team more frequently."

Always end with: "Here is how I am actively working on it." Growth mindset wins panels.

Before you walk in

Mindset

  • You are being evaluated, not judged. The panel wants to find their next hire.
  • Nerves are energy - redirect them. Breathe slowly, sit tall, let it fuel your delivery.
  • You have lived experiences they cannot teach. That is what they are looking for.
  • One bad answer does not sink you. Keep moving forward after every question.

First impression

  • The interview starts in the parking lot. Assume someone is watching from the moment you arrive.
  • Firm handshake with every panel member. Eye contact. Smile.
  • Walk in like you belong there - confident and calm, not cocky.
  • Introduce yourself clearly: "Good morning, I'm Matthew Jones. Thank you for the opportunity."

Body language

  • Sit up straight from the moment you sit down. Do not slouch into comfort.
  • Make eye contact with ALL panel members - not just whoever asked the question.
  • Do not cross your arms. Keep hands visible and relaxed.
  • Lean in slightly when answering - shows engagement and energy.
  • Do not fidget, tap, or touch anything on the table.

Voice and delivery

  • Speak louder than a normal conversation - confident, not yelling.
  • Be energetic. Sell the emotion behind your stories. Monotone loses panels fast.
  • Slow down. Nervous people speed up. Take a breath before each answer.
  • It is okay to say "Can I take a moment to think about that?" before answering.
  • Cut filler words: "um," "like," "you know," "kind of." Replace them with a pause.

Answering well

  • Every answer needs a personal story. Define it, tell the story with SARA, tie it to fire service.
  • Keep answers under 3 minutes. If you are still talking at 4 minutes, you have lost them.
  • Do not use the same story twice. Have multiple real experiences ready.
  • If you do not know something, say so - then bridge to what you DO know.
  • End every answer strong. Do not trail off. Land the plane confidently.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Saying "I want to help people" as your primary why.
  • Answering with "we" instead of "I" - they are hiring you, not your past team.
  • Claiming you have no weaknesses or have never had a conflict.
  • Badmouthing former employers, coaches, or coworkers.
  • Not having questions to ask at the end - signals low interest and preparation.
  • Forgetting to acknowledge every panel member with eye contact during your answer.
After the interview

When it's over: Thank each panel member individually. Firm handshake. Walk out composed - not deflated or overly excited.

Debrief immediately: As soon as you get to your car, write down every question you were asked. This is gold for Springdale prep on June 26.

Follow-up: A brief thank-you email within 24 hours is a strong move. Keep it short - thank them for their time, restate your interest, one sentence on why you are excited about this department. Do not beg or over-explain.
